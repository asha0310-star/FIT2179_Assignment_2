A Crop That Conquered the World

In 60 years, palm oil became the world's most-traded vegetable oil. Malaysia and Indonesia drove almost all of that growth. Today, they produce 85% of the world's crude palm oil. That rise brought both economic gains and environmental cost.

The chart above shows the long rise of Malaysia and Indonesia, while the other producers stay small. Below, the bar chart ranks the largest producers and the line chart shows Malaysia's shrinking share. That share fell from 76% in 1982 to 27% in 2023, even as output kept rising.

Data source: Our World in Data — Palm Oil. Malaysia and Indonesia dominate global production. Click a country in the legend to isolate its line. "Other" combines all remaining producers.

Malaysia remains the world's second-largest palm oil producer, but its global share is falling. In 1990 it made more than 70% of the world's palm oil; by 2023 that had dropped to 27%. The industry is still growing, but faster growth elsewhere is pushing Malaysia to expand into new land.

From a Few Estates to Millions of Hectares

In 1961, Malaysia had fewer than 55,000 hectares of oil palm. By 2024, that had grown to 5.6 million hectares. Most of the expansion came after the 1980s and sped up again after 2000. The key question is where that land came from.

Data source: Our World in Data. The slope chart compares the start and end of the trend. Indonesia now has more oil palm area than Malaysia. "Other" includes emerging producers in Central Africa, Central America, and Papua New Guinea.

Data source: Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB) 2024 Overview. Planted area and production move together closely. Productivity per hectare has stayed broadly stable for two decades. Most production growth comes from more planted area.

Data source: MPOB 2024 Overview. Until the 1990s, Peninsular Malaysia was the country's main palm oil region. Since 2000, Sabah and Sarawak have grown rapidly and now produce more than 60% of Malaysia's crude palm oil. That shift matters because East Malaysia still contains large tracts of primary forest.

The Forest Pays the Bill

Between 2001 and 2024, Malaysia lost roughly 9.5 million hectares of tree cover. The losses are concentrated in the same states where palm oil expanded fastest: Sarawak, Sabah, and parts of the Peninsula. Forest loss peaked in 2012 and 2014, during years of intense plantation development. Tree cover loss includes commercial harvest and plantation rotations, not only clearing of primary forest.

Data sources: MPOB (planted area) and Global Forest Watch (tree cover loss). Each dot is one year from 2001 to 2024. The scatter plot shows how years with more planted area also tend to have higher forest loss. The later years drift down and right, suggesting a possible decoupling.

Data source: Global Forest Watch, 2001–2024. Darker shades of orange indicate higher cumulative tree cover loss. Sarawak and Sabah together account for more than half of Malaysia's forest loss. Peninsular states have lower absolute loss but still show high relative pressure.

Palm oil plantations in Malaysia were established predominantly on land that was previously forested. The overlap between high-production states and high forest-loss states is not coincidence. It is the same land, before and after. Data sources: Global Forest Watch (forest loss 2001–2024); MPOB Annual Overview 2024 (state CPO production).

The heatmap shows the full pattern of forest loss across states and years. Sarawak and Sabah stand out as the darkest rows in the 2010s. The national trend peaks in 2012 and 2014, and then declines after 2015.

Data source: Global Forest Watch. Each panel shows one Malaysian state's annual tree cover loss over 24 years. States are sorted by total loss, largest to smallest. Sarawak and Sabah show the most volatile patterns, while Peninsular states are more moderate. The national trend hides those regional differences.

Where the Oil Goes

In 2024, Malaysia exported 16.9 million tonnes of palm oil worth RM73 billion. That makes palm oil a cornerstone of the national economy. But demand is concentrated: seven countries buy more than half of Malaysia's exports. That concentration adds pressure to keep expanding plantations.

Data source: MPOB 2024 Overview (trade data). The top seven importers (India, European Union, China, Kenya, Turkiye, the Philippines, and Japan) buy 54.2% of Malaysia's palm oil exports. India is the largest importer. Demand in these markets comes from food, biofuel, and industrial uses.

What Comes Next

Malaysia's palm oil boom has created jobs, export revenue, and rural development. It has also driven one of the world's fastest rates of deforestation. The country has started to respond through replanting and stricter certification. Tree cover loss has declined since 2014.

Whether forest cover can recover depends on how Malaysia treats palm oil land going forward. If replanted estates are treated as development land, recovery is unlikely. If certification and enforcement protect remaining forests, production and forest loss may decouple. The next decade will decide which path wins.
