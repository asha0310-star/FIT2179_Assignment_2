Forests for Palm Oil

How a single crop reshaped a country's landscape and where the cost has fallen hardest.

Designed for a general Malaysian audience, this visualisation explains how palm oil growth connects to land expansion, forest loss, and export demand.

A Crop That Conquered the World

Palm oil became the world's dominant vegetable oil in just six decades. Malaysia helped drive that boom, but the crop also pushed plantation land deeper into forested regions. This story follows the trade-off: more oil, more land, and forest loss concentrated in Sabah and Sarawak.

The first chart shows how Malaysia and Indonesia separated from the rest of the world. The bar chart ranks producers by year, while the line chart shows Malaysia's shrinking global share. Malaysia's share fell from 76% in 1982 to 27% in 2023, even as output kept rising.

Data source: Our World in Data — Palm Oil. Malaysia and Indonesia dominate global production. Click a country in the legend to isolate its line. "Other" combines all remaining producers.

Malaysia remains the world's second-largest palm oil producer, but its global share is falling. In 1990 it made more than 70% of the world's palm oil; by 2023 that had dropped to 27%. The industry is still growing, but faster growth elsewhere is pushing Malaysia to expand into new land.

From a Few Estates to Millions of Hectares

In 1961, Malaysia had fewer than 55,000 hectares of oil palm. By 2024, that had grown to 5.6 million hectares. Most of the expansion came after the 1980s and sped up again after 2000. The key question is where that land came from.

Data source: Our World in Data. The slope chart compares the start and end of the trend. Indonesia now has more oil palm area than Malaysia. "Other" includes emerging producers in Central Africa, Central America, and Papua New Guinea.

Data sources: MPOB 2024 Overview and Global Forest Watch. This derived metric divides annual tree-cover loss by Malaysia's crude palm oil production. Lower values mean fewer thousand hectares of tree cover loss per million tonnes of CPO produced. The decline after 2014 suggests partial decoupling, but it does not prove that palm oil no longer creates forest pressure.

Data source: MPOB 2024 Overview. Until the 1990s, Peninsular Malaysia was the country's main palm oil region. Since 2000, Sabah and Sarawak have grown rapidly and now produce more than 60% of Malaysia's crude palm oil. That shift matters because East Malaysia still contains large tracts of primary forest.

The Forest Pays the Bill

Between 2001 and 2024, Malaysia lost roughly 9.5 million hectares of tree cover. The largest losses were concentrated in Sarawak, Sabah, and parts of the Peninsula. Forest loss peaked in 2012 and 2014, around years of intense plantation development. Tree-cover loss includes commercial harvest and plantation rotations, not only clearing of primary forest.

Data sources: MPOB (planted area) and Global Forest Watch (tree cover loss). Each dot is one year from 2001 to 2024. The scatter plot shows how years with more planted area also tend to have higher forest loss. The later years drift down and right, suggesting a possible decoupling.

Data source: Global Forest Watch, 2001–2024. Darker shades of orange indicate higher cumulative tree-cover loss in absolute hectares. The map highlights where Malaysia's total forest-loss burden is concentrated. Because larger states can naturally have larger totals, read it together with the state-level time series below.

Palm oil expansion and tree-cover loss are concentrated in many of the same states, especially Sabah and Sarawak. This does not prove that every lost hectare became plantation land, but it shows where economic expansion and forest pressure overlap most strongly. Data sources: Global Forest Watch (forest loss 2001–2024); MPOB Annual Overview 2024 (state CPO production).

Where the Oil Goes

In 2024, Malaysia exported 16.9 million tonnes of palm oil worth RM73 billion. That makes palm oil a cornerstone of the national economy. But demand is concentrated: seven countries buy more than half of Malaysia's exports. That concentration adds pressure to keep expanding plantations.

Data source: MPOB 2024 Overview (trade data). The top seven importers (India, European Union, China, Kenya, Turkiye, the Philippines, and Japan) buy 54.2% of Malaysia's palm oil exports. India is the largest importer. Demand in these markets comes from food, biofuel, and industrial uses.

What Comes Next

Malaysia's palm oil boom has created jobs, export revenue, and rural development. It has also contributed to severe forest pressure in the regions where expansion was concentrated. The country has started to respond through replanting and stricter certification. Tree cover loss has declined since 2014.

Whether forest cover can recover depends on how Malaysia treats palm oil land going forward. If replanted estates are treated as development land, recovery is unlikely. If certification and enforcement protect remaining forests, production and forest loss may decouple. The next decade will decide which path wins.
