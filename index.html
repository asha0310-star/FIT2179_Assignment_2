A Crop That Conquered the World

In sixty years, palm oil grew from a small West African export into the world's most-traded vegetable oil. Malaysia and Indonesia produced almost all of that growth. Today, these two countries make 85% of the world's crude palm oil — a position achieved through decades of continuous expansion. The story of Malaysian palm oil is one of economic ambition and environmental cost.

The chart above traces sixty years of expansion: Malaysia and Indonesia (orange and dark green) carry the global trajectory, while every other producer combined (grey) barely moves the dial. Below, the snapshot on the left ranks today's largest producers, and the line on the right tracks how Malaysia's share of that growing pie has slipped from 76 % in 1982 to 27 % in 2023 — even as its absolute output kept climbing.

Data source: Our World in Data — Palm Oil. Malaysia (orange) and Indonesia (dark green) dominate global production. Click a country in the legend to isolate its line; "Other" combines all remaining producers.

While Malaysia remains the world's second-largest palm oil producer after Indonesia, its share of global production is declining. In 1990 Malaysia made more than 70% of the world's palm oil; by 2023 this had dropped to just 27%. This reflects not a shrinking Malaysian industry, but a rapidly expanding one in Indonesia and other producers in Southeast Asia. For Malaysia, maintaining production levels while losing market share creates pressure to expand into new land.

From a Few Estates to Millions of Hectares

In 1961 fewer than 55,000 hectares of Malaysia were planted with oil palm — an area smaller than a major city. By 2024 the figure was 5.6 million hectares (5.6 Mha), an area roughly the size of Croatia. Most of this expansion happened after the 1980s, and it has accelerated sharply since 2000. The question is not whether Malaysia has expanded, but where the land came from.

Data source: Our World in Data. Slope charts show the beginning and ending of a trend. Indonesia now has more oil palm area than Malaysia, having started much smaller. The "Other" category includes emerging producers in Central Africa, Central America, and Papua New Guinea.

Data source: Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB) 2024 Overview. The tight correlation between planted area (green line) and production (orange bars) shows that productivity per hectare has been stable for two decades. Growth in production is driven almost entirely by growth in planted area.

Data source: MPOB 2024 Overview. Until the 1990s, Peninsular Malaysia was the country's main palm oil region. Since 2000, Sabah and Sarawak (East Malaysia) have grown rapidly and now produce more than 60% of Malaysia's crude palm oil. This shift is significant because East Malaysia still contains large tracts of primary forest.

The Forest Pays the Bill

Between 2001 and 2024 Malaysia lost roughly 9.5 million hectares of tree cover — about 29% of the country's total land area. These losses are not random or evenly distributed. They are concentrated in the same states where palm oil expanded the fastest: Sarawak, Sabah, and parts of the Peninsula. The timing is telling: forest loss peaked in 2012 and 2014, years of intense plantation development. Tree cover loss includes commercial harvest and plantation rotations, not only clearing of primary forest (see Global Forest Watch methodology).

Data sources: MPOB (planted area) and Global Forest Watch (tree cover loss). Each dot represents one year (2001–2024). The path shows how forest loss has evolved as Malaysia's planted area increased. The recent trend (rightward, lower) suggests that forest loss is beginning to decouple from plantation expansion — possibly due to land saturation or stricter enforcement of sustainability rules (MSPO mandatory from 2020).

Data source: Global Forest Watch, 2001–2024. Darker shades of orange indicate higher cumulative tree cover loss. Sarawak (2.9M ha) and Sabah (2.1M ha) together account for more than half of Malaysia's forest loss. Peninsular states have lower absolute loss but high relative loss compared to remaining forest area.

Palm oil plantations in Malaysia were established predominantly on land that was previously forested. The spatial overlap between high-production states (Sabah, Sarawak) and high forest-loss states is not coincidence — it is the same land, before and after. Data sources: Global Forest Watch (forest loss 2001–2024); MPOB Annual Overview 2024 (state CPO production).

The heatmap (left) shows that forest loss was concentrated in Sarawak and Sabah in the 2010s. The national trend (right) peaks in 2012 (~628 kha) and 2014 (~646 kha) — years of aggressive expansion driven by rising commodity prices and heavy investment in East Malaysian concessions. Since 2015, annual losses have fallen, but the cumulative damage is substantial.

Data source: Global Forest Watch. Each panel shows one Malaysian state's annual tree cover loss over 24 years. States are sorted by total loss (largest to smallest). Sarawak and Sabah stand out for their volatile, peak-heavy patterns, whereas Peninsular states show more moderate losses. This detail reveals that the national trend masks very different regional stories.

Where the Oil Goes

In 2024 Malaysia exported 16.9 million tonnes (16.9 Mt) of palm oil worth RM73 billion — a cornerstone of the national economy and a vital source of foreign exchange. But the markets for this oil are concentrated: just seven countries buy more than half of Malaysia's exports. This concentration matters because it affects demand growth and, consequently, the pressure to expand plantations further.

Data source: MPOB 2024 Overview (trade data). The top seven importers (India, European Union, China, Kenya, Turkiye, the Philippines, and Japan) collectively buy 54.2% of Malaysia's palm oil exports. India remains the largest importer, and demand in these markets is driven by food production, biofuel mandates, and industrial applications. Rising demand in these markets has historically driven expansion in Malaysia and Indonesia.

What Comes Next

Malaysia's palm oil boom has created genuine economic value — jobs, export revenue, and rural development. But it has also driven one of the world's fastest rates of deforestation. The country has begun to acknowledge this trade-off: it has replanted older estates and tightened sustainability certification (MSPO became mandatory in 2020). Tree cover loss has declined since 2014.

Whether forest cover can truly recover depends on how Malaysia treats palm oil land going forward. If the industry treats replanted estates as already converted land (available for re-development), forest recovery is unlikely. But if certification schemes and enforcement genuinely protect remaining forests, and if demand growth in key markets moderates, there is a path toward decoupling production from forest loss. The next decade will determine which story Malaysia tells.
