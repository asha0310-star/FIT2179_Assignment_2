Forests for Palm Oil

How a single crop reshaped a country's landscape and where the cost has fallen hardest.

Designed for a general Malaysian audience, this visualisation explains how palm oil growth connects to land expansion, tree-cover loss, and export demand.

A Crop That Conquered the World

Palm oil became the world's dominant vegetable oil in just six decades. Malaysia helped drive that boom, but the crop also pushed plantation land deeper into forested regions. This story follows the trade-off: more oil, more land, and tree-cover loss concentrated in Sabah and Sarawak.

The first chart shows how Malaysia and Indonesia separated from the rest of the world. The bar chart ranks major crude palm oil producers by year, while the line chart shows Malaysia's shrinking share in the longer Our World in Data series. Malaysia's share peaked at about 57% in 1982 and had fallen to about 23% by 2023, even as its own output kept rising.

Data source: Our World in Data — Palm Oil. Malaysia and Indonesia dominate global production. Click a country in the legend to isolate its line. “Other” combines all remaining producers.

Malaysia remains the world's second-largest palm oil producer, but its global share is falling because Indonesia and other producers expanded faster. This means Malaysia can produce more oil while still becoming less dominant globally. The pressure then shifts from being “how much can Malaysia grow?” to “how can Malaysia grow without losing more forest?”

From a Few Estates to Millions of Hectares

In 1961, Malaysia had fewer than 55,000 hectares of oil palm. By 2025, MPOB reported 5.70 million hectares planted with oil palm. Most of the expansion came after the 1980s and sped up again after 2000. The key question is where that land came from.

Data source: Our World in Data. The slope chart compares the start and end of the available land-use trend. Indonesia now has more oil palm area than Malaysia. “Other” includes emerging producers in Central Africa, Central America, and Papua New Guinea.

Data sources: MPOB 2024 Overview and Global Forest Watch. This derived metric divides annual tree-cover loss by Malaysia's crude palm oil production. Lower values mean fewer thousand hectares of tree cover loss per million tonnes of CPO produced. The decline after 2014 suggests partial decoupling, but it does not prove that palm oil no longer creates forest pressure.

Data source: MPOB 2025 Overview. Peninsular Malaysia still produces the largest regional share of CPO, while Sabah and Sarawak together produce 43.9%. That regional split matters because the largest cumulative tree-cover losses are concentrated in East Malaysia.

The Forest Pays the Bill

Between 2001 and 2024, Malaysia lost roughly 9.5 million hectares of tree cover. The largest losses were concentrated in Sarawak, Sabah, and parts of the Peninsula. Forest loss peaked in 2012 and 2014, around years of intense plantation development. Tree-cover loss includes commercial harvest and plantation rotations, not only clearing of primary forest.

Data sources: MPOB planted-area series and Global Forest Watch tree-cover loss. Each dot is one year from 2001 to 2024. The scatter plot shows that years with more planted area often had higher tree-cover loss, but this is a relationship, not proof that every lost hectare became oil palm.

Data source: Global Forest Watch, 2001–2024. Darker shades of orange indicate higher cumulative tree-cover loss in absolute hectares. Because larger states can naturally have larger totals, read it together with the state-level time series below rather than as a per-hectare rate.

Palm oil production and tree-cover loss are concentrated in overlapping parts of Malaysia, especially Sabah and Sarawak. This does not prove that every lost hectare became plantation land, but it shows where economic production and forest pressure overlap most strongly. Data sources: Global Forest Watch (forest loss 2001–2024); MPOB 2025 Overview (regional CPO production).

Export value climbed while annual forest loss fell from its 2012–2014 peaks.

Data sources: MPOB Annual Overview reports and Global Forest Watch. Bars above zero show export value in RM billions. Bars below zero show annual tree-cover loss in thousand hectares, rescaled only to compare year-by-year direction, not direct equivalence between money and land. 2008 marks the global financial crisis; 2012 and 2014 mark forest-loss peaks; 2022 marks the export-value record.

The heatmap shows the full pattern of forest loss across states and years. Sarawak and Sabah stand out as the darkest rows in the 2010s. The national trend peaks in 2012 and 2014, and then declines after 2015.

Data source: Global Forest Watch. Each panel shows one Malaysian state's annual tree-cover loss over 24 years. States are sorted by total loss, largest to smallest. Sarawak and Sabah show the most volatile patterns, while Peninsular states are more moderate. The national trend hides those regional differences.

Where the Oil Goes

In 2025, Malaysia exported 15.27 million tonnes of palm oil worth RM71.63 billion. Palm oil remains a cornerstone of the national economy, even though export volume fell from 2024. Demand is concentrated: seven markets bought 51.5% of Malaysia's palm oil exports. That concentration adds pressure to keep production reliable while meeting stricter sustainability expectations.

Data source: MPOB 2025 Overview. The top seven importers — India, Kenya, the European Union, China, Türkiye, the Philippines, and Japan — bought 7.86 million tonnes, or 51.5% of Malaysia's palm oil exports. India remained the largest importer.

What Comes Next

Malaysia's palm oil boom has created jobs, export revenue, and rural development. It has also contributed to severe forest pressure in the regions where expansion was concentrated. The country has started to respond through replanting, certification, and stronger land-use controls. Tree-cover loss has declined since 2014, but the remaining forest is still valuable and vulnerable.

Whether forest cover can recover depends on how Malaysia treats palm oil land going forward. If replanted estates are treated as development land, recovery is unlikely. If certification and enforcement protect remaining forests, production and forest loss may continue to decouple. The next decade will decide which path wins.
